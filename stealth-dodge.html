Continuous Motor Tracking & Spatial Collision Avoidance Under Stress

By Dr. DON'T BLINK Research Staff • Reading time: 5 minutes • Kinematic Analysis & High-Speed Evasion

While discrete reaction time games measure a single instantaneous impulse, real-world high-stakes tasks—such as high-speed driving, aviation dogfighting, competitive esports, and athletic dodging—demand continuous closed-loop motor control. Stealth Dodge benchmarks this exact neurobiological capability.

The Cerebellar Feedback Loop in Closed-Loop Tracking

Continuous spatial dodging relies on the cerebellum and motor cortex performing micro-corrections every 50 to 100 milliseconds. As hazards fall and oscillate, your ocular system computes time-to-contact ($\tau$) predictions, sending real-time efferent signals to the hand to maintain a safe positional buffer.

Fitts's Law and Spatial Micro-Adjustments

Under intense cognitive stress, human operators frequently over-steer or jerk their controls—a phenomenon known as motor oscillation. In Stealth Dodge, panic-swiping often causes collision with secondary hazards. The elite players who survive beyond 40 seconds employ smooth pursuit tracking and minimal micro-movements, preserving central positioning rather than fleeing erratically to screen corners.

Pro Tips for Maximizing Survival Time

  • Maintain Center Gravity: Never hug the screen boundaries. Hazards cluster along the edges, trapping you in dead ends. Always navigate back to the center third of the arena.
  • Eyes on Hazards, Not Your Craft: Rely on peripheral vision for your ship's coordinates. Focus your foveal vision 100–150 pixels ahead to identify incoming escape lanes before hazards converge.
  • Exploit Grazing Physics: Skimming hazards within 12 pixels awards Graze points and boosts your final ranking without triggering collision hulls.
